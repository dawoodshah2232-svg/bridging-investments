Sponsor alignment: why we invest our own capital alongside you.

8 Oct 2026 · By the OwnStakeX team

There is an old rule in investing: follow where the sponsor's own money goes. We invest our own capital alongside our investors — and here is why that matters, and where its limits are.

A "sponsor" is the party that finds the asset, structures the deal, and manages it. In many investment structures, the sponsor earns fees for its work but puts none of its own money at risk. That creates an uneven relationship: if the project underperforms, investors lose capital while the sponsor has only lost time. When a sponsor co-invests — committing its own capital on the same terms as everyone else — the relationship changes. Success and failure are shared, not merely distributed.

What co-investment actually aligns

Alignment is not a slogan; it is a set of practical effects. A sponsor with its own money in the deal tends to underwrite more carefully, because the downside is personal. It tends to negotiate harder on purchase price and operating costs, because every dirham saved benefits its own position too. And it tends to manage problems faster, because a struggling asset hurts its own balance sheet, not just its reputation.

There is a subtler effect as well: co-investment disciplines the pitch. A sponsor that must commit its own capital alongside yours cannot afford to believe its own marketing. The projects that get launched are the ones the sponsor would back with its own money — which is exactly the filter an investor would choose, if given one.

What co-investment does not do

Honesty requires the limits to be stated plainly. Co-investment does not remove risk — a well-aligned sponsor can still pick a project that underperforms, and your capital remains at risk. It does not guarantee returns of any kind; alignment of incentives is not a promise of outcomes. And the size of the co-investment matters: a token amount alongside a large fee stream aligns little. When you evaluate any sponsor's claim of alignment, ask two questions: how much of its own money is in, and on exactly whose terms?

  • The amount: is the co-investment meaningful relative to the fees the sponsor earns?
  • The terms: does the sponsor invest on the same share class, the same price, and the same risk as you — or on preferential terms?
  • The lock-in: can the sponsor sell out early while you are committed, or does it stay in for the same holding period?

How to use this as an investor

Treat sponsor alignment as one input among several, not as a verdict. Read the co-investment terms in the project's documents — the amount, the class of shares, and any difference in terms should all be disclosed. A sponsor that answers these questions directly, with numbers, is showing you the same transparency it asks of its projects.

We publish our co-investment position in every project's documents because we believe you should be able to check our alignment, not take it on faith. The principle is simple: we should never ask you to take a risk we would not take ourselves.
